Rules vs. Laws, and the Property Clause Theory of Administrative Law

By Charles A. Iantorno, Esq. October 1, 2026

Working Paper

Abstract

In a 1975 Harvard Law Review article, Professor Richard Stewart described a war in US administrative law causing “exigent difficulties” and “dense intellectual and social burdens.” See Richard B. Stewart, The Reformation of American Administrative Law, 88 Harv. L. Rev. 1667 (1975). Professor Stewart argued that the two dominant theories of US administrative law, the unitary executive theory and the public interest or independent agency theories, were “untenable” and “inconsistent.” Id. Almost a half century later, the Yale Law School Sterling Professor Emeritus Jerry L. Mashaw largely agreed with Professor Stewart, stating that a consistent, general theory of administrative legitimacy “still eludes us.” See Jerry L. Mashaw, Is Administrative Law at War with Itself?, 29 N.Y.U. Envtl. L.J. 421 (2021). To Professor Mashaw, the post-1975 Supreme Court cases also seemed “deeply contradictory,” “unresolvable,” and “a muddle.” Id.

The first issue is that both dominant theories, and the current Supreme Court caselaw, allow Congress to delegate the power to define, specify, or prescribe what acts by private citizens are or are not illegal. It is widely accepted in the field of US administrative law that agency “rules” may bind US citizens with the force of law. The Court has long held Congress must be permitted to let others “fill in the details,” so long as the law provides a sufficient “intelligible principle” for the agencies to follow. More recently, the Court explained that Congress may delegate the authority to decide “major questions,” but only if Congress does so clearly or unambiguously. Meanwhile, the Court has repeated, throughout the decades, in one form or another, the “axiom” that Congress may not delegate its legislative authority. This is one patent inconsistency in our law.

One problem is the Constitution already distinguishes between rules and laws, and the distinction matters. The lawmaking or legislative power is the authority to prescribe what acts or omissions are or are not illegal. Laws bind everyone in the jurisdiction. That is why the federal lawmaking process, and the reach or scope of federal laws, are more circumscribed than its rulemaking powers. Congress’ rulemaking powers come from Article IV, § 3, cl. 2: “[t]he Congress shall have power to dispose of and make all needful rules and regulations respecting the territory or other property belonging to the United States” (“the Property Clause”). As Secretary of the Treasury Alexander Hamilton noted in his Reports of 1791, the power to make rules for federal property or asset management would naturally include the power to let others fill in the details, to enable the effective operation of the rule or management plan. This differs from laws because rules for federal property do not bind everyone of the land. Exercising rules over federal property is not the same as exercising “exclusive” legislative police powers.

The text and history confirm that Congress may flexibly delegate rulemaking authority over its own assets. That is because the acceptance, rejection, or ownership claim over an asset or license to federal property is not a question of law. It’s a question about rules, where the individual chose to engage with the US property or US property manager. The dispute is usually in rem, between the individual and the United States, covered by the Article III judicial power, in both law and equity. The particular claim process does not bind everyone of the land; it’s only a question about who owns title to that property or license. The disposal and management of federal assets is separate and apart from creating, executing, and adjudicating a law.

By bifurcating between rules and laws, many tough questions of administrative law can be more easily slotted into their proper channels. Laws must be made by the legislature, must accord with the Constitution, and must provide fair notice of what is or is not illegal. Rules are more flexible, but the rulemaking power is not absolute. The rule or its application must be “needful.”

The Property Clause Theory of Administrative Law leaves many legal, historical, and moral questions unanswered. But the “exigent” crisis in the field of pure US administrative legal theory is indeed resolvable; the logical “muddle” is not so complex, and it need not burden us any longer. The Supreme Court just needs to properly distinguish between rules and laws and explain why the text says what it says. Rules bind those who choose to engage with federal property. Laws bind everyone whether they consent or not. That is why the legislative branch must pass proper legislation before the executive branch investigates, takes from, or arrests individuals. Finally, individuals, judges, and juries must be able to tell by reading the U.S. Code whether federal law was violated.
